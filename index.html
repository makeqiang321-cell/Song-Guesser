What Is Song Guesser?

Song Guesser runs on one idea: you need less of a song than you think to place it. No trivia, no artwork, no year to lean on.

In Song Guesser you work from sound alone, typing the title the moment the recording clicks. It's a Heardle-style song guessing game, only faster: guess the song from the shortest clip you can, and leave the longer reveals sitting there untouched until you actually need them.

How to Play Song Guesser

Press play in Song Guesser, hear whatever tenth of a second you get, and decide on the spot: type it now, or burn a reveal for a little more of the track.

  1. 01▶

    Listen to the sliver

    Song Guesser gives you a tenth of a second, played once. Headphones help here — a drum fill or a vocal breath is often the whole clue, but only if you're listening for it.

  2. 02⌕

    Name the track

    Type any part of the title and let Song Guesser's suggestions finish the rest. A rhythm, a melody, or the way the snare was recorded can hand you the answer before the clip does.

  3. 03⏭

    Widen only when stuck

    Wrong answer or skip? The clip steps up to 0.5s, 2s, 8s, then 15s. Each step makes it easier, so the goal is to beat the song from the shortest clip you can — that's the whole point of Song Guesser.

Sound before words

Why can you guess the song in 0.1 seconds?

Song Guesser hands you a tenth of a second: no lyrics, no full melody. But a track you know carries a signature — the snare's attack, the grain of a voice, how big the room sounds, the synth's exact timbre. Any one of those can name the song before you say it. A longer clip adds nothing. It just repeats what that first instant already told you: rhythm, harmony, and the melody only at the end.

What's actually in 0.1 seconds of a song?

Less than a note. At 120 beats per minute one beat lasts half a second, so a tenth of a second is a fifth of a beat — shorter than a sixteenth note and about the length of a single syllable. No melody and no word can fit inside it.

What survives is timbre: the sound of this exact recording, not the song as written. The instruments, the voice, the way it was produced. A cover can't reproduce it.

  • December 1999 — Schellenberg, Iverson & McKinnon (Psychonomic Bulletin & Review) asked listeners to match five hit-song clips to a list of five titles. On 0.2-second clips, 19 of 20 beat chance; on 0.1-second clips, everyone still beat chance. Reversed, or with everything above 1000 Hz removed, they dropped back to guessing. The caveat the authors wrote out plainly: the songs were few and known in advance, and people don't normally name a song from this little.
  • June 2010 — Krumhansl (Music Perception) had Cornell students name artist and title from 0.4-second clips of 1960s-to-2000s hits, and they got it right 26.4% of the time. Cut the clip to 0.3 seconds and accuracy fell to 11.1%. An open answer is far harder than five choices.
  • June 2014 — a study in the Journal of the Acoustical Society of America found listeners can tell a voice from an instrument in clips only a few thousandths of a second long. Timbre is read that fast.
  • 2019 — a University College London team tracked pupils as people heard songs they knew. The eye reacted 0.1–0.3 seconds after the first sound, before the listener could name anything.

That last number is the one the game runs on. A tenth of a second sits at the very edge of what your ears register, which is why the clip grows — 0.5s, 2s, 8s, 15s — and you name it the moment your hunch turns solid.

That's why every round opens easy and climbs. Start on songs everyone knows, then reach for rarer cuts once your ear catches up. Five levels mean a casual listener and a deep-cut fan can both play without sharing a song pool.

A listening method

How to get better at the 0.1-second guess

Do these four things before you press play.

  1. 01👂

    Know it before you name it

    Ask "have I heard this?" first, not "what is this?" A yes narrows the field to songs you actually know; a no tells you to widen instead of guessing blind.

  2. 02🎧

    Listen to the top of the sound

    There's no melody in the first sliver, so don't wait for one. The onset carries the whole identity: the drummer's strike, where the voice sits in the mix, how far away the room feels. Read that, not the tune.

  3. 03🎤

    Wait for a syllable

    Most voices give the singer away in the first word. If nothing sings, lean on the synth or guitar tone instead.

  4. 04⏫

    Widen in small steps

    Move 0.1s to 0.5s to 2s, not straight to 15s. Each step is cheap and either confirms your hunch or kills it.
